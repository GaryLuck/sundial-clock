The Science Behind Sundials

How a Horizontal Sundial Works

A horizontal sundial consists of a flat plate (the dial face) marked with hours, and a central triangular blade called the gnomon. As the Sun travels across the sky from East to West, the gnomon casts a dark shadow onto the dial plate.

Unlike the hands of an analog clock which sweep at a constant speed of 15° per hour, a sundial's hour lines are unequally spaced! This non-linear layout is because we are projecting a circular solar orbit onto a flat horizontal plate.

The Latitude Alignment

To ensure that the shadow falls on the correct hour line throughout the entire year, the gnomon's sloped edge must be perfectly parallel to the Earth's axis of rotation. Therefore, the angle of the gnomon slope must equal the exact latitude of its location! Adjust the latitude slider to observe how the dial face automatically restructures itself for different cities.

Mathematical Projection Formula

The angle $R$ of each hour line (relative to North) is computed dynamically using spherical trigonometry:

tan(R) = sin(Latitude) × tan(h)

Where h is the solar hour angle (15° for each hour before or after Solar Noon). This explains why hour lines bunch up closer to the 12:00 PM noon line at lower latitudes, and spread wider apart at high polar latitudes!

What is a Moondial?

A moondial is an instrument that tells time using the moonlight shadow cast by a gnomon. Because the moon orbits the Earth, its position shifts by about 48 minutes later each night compared to the sun. A standard sundial can only be used as a moondial during a Full Moon when the Moon is directly opposite the Sun in the night sky!
